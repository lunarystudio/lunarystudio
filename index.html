01  The output

Not a line. Not a season. Not a set collection. Four kinds of work — and no rule about how they combine.

Garments

Cut and sewn one at a time from deadstock, mill ends and vintage cloth taken apart for its material. One size, drafted for one body.

Journals

Hand-bound books — stitched signatures, cloth or leather covers, paper chosen for how it takes ink. Made to be written in, not displayed.

Art prints

Single pulls and one-off prints, screened or pressed by hand in the studio. No edition of fifty. One impression, signed and dated.

Objects

The rest of it: engraved goods, small runs of one, experiments that turned out too good not to release. Whatever the bench produces.

The ruleA drop is whatever is finished when it's time to release — one garment, four journals, a print and a box of small things. I don't plan the mix and I won't announce it in advance. The only constant is that nothing repeats.

02 The mechanic

How a drop works

Four steps, no cart drama. The quantity is a surprise; the rules aren't.

01 / Made

A piece gets finished.

Sourced, built and photographed. Photographed hours before release, never weeks — no pre-orders, no promises.

02 / Released

It goes live quietly.

Whatever is ready, goes up together — one piece or a dozen. No preview, no countdown, no contents list beforehand. It's just there.

03 / Claimed

Whoever gets there, gets it.

Each piece is a single unit. First checkout on that piece wins. No holds, no DMs asking to reserve it until Friday.

04 / Closed

None of it returns.

Every piece goes into the gallery marked sold. No re-run in another colourway, no second printing. That's what "one of one" has to mean to be worth saying.

03 The work

Gallery

Everything that's left the studio, in the order it happened. Nothing removed once it's here.

04 The hand

How it's made

Small enough that a single piece can take three weeks. That's the point.

01

Materials first, mostly secondhand

Deadstock cloth, mill-end paper, vintage frames and findings. What I can find decides what gets made.

02

One at a time, by hand

A garment cut and sewn. A book stitched and bound. A print pulled and dried. No batch, no production run.

03

Numbered and moon-dated

Every release gets a stamped number and the moon phase of the day it was finished — the same mark on the piece and in the gallery.

04

Photographed the day it ships

Shot once, in daylight, in its real state. What you see in the drop is the exact object that arrives at your door.

07 The fine print

Questions

When is the next drop? +

When the piece is finished. I don't announce dates I can't guarantee — the drop alert goes out the day it goes live, so the people who asked to be told are told first.

How many pieces are in a drop? +

No fixed number. Sometimes one garment that took three weeks. Sometimes six things at once because they all happened to finish in the same month. I don't build drops to a count, and I won't tell you what's in one before it's live — only that every piece in it is one of one.

What kinds of things do you make? +

Garments, hand-bound journals, art prints and small objects — in whatever combination a drop turns out to be. The gallery is the honest record of what's actually come out of here, including how many pieces each drop held.

Can you make one for me? +

A one-of-one is built once, for one person. If a piece isn't your size, it isn't yours. Commission work is a separate conversation — email the studio rather than waiting for a drop.

Will sold pieces come back? +

No. No restocks, no second colourway, no reprint "by popular demand." The gallery stays exactly as it is, which is why it's worth looking at.

Where do I actually buy? +

Checkout happens in the shop, not on this page. When a drop is live, the button at the top of this site goes straight to that piece's listing. The gallery is a record, not a cart — tiles link to listings only while something is available.

Shipping and returns? +

Ships within 3 business days of purchase, tracked, US and international. Because each piece is unique, returns are accepted within 7 days of delivery, unused — but a return means the piece is gone for good.

Three weeks for one vest

Where the time actually goes, hour by hour, and why I stopped apologizing for it.

People ask how long a piece takes and I can hear the follow-up in the question: that long? for one thing? The first drop hasn't left the bench yet, so here is the breakdown of the piece in progress — the honest shape of it, not a projection.

Finding the material — days, spread over weeks. Not days of work: days of driving, digging, and coming home with nothing. The current piece is built from a quilt top that sat in a shop bin for decades. I looked at it three separate times before committing, because I couldn't see a garment in it yet. That's the part that can't be scheduled.

Taking it apart — hours of unpicking. Every seam by hand. A quilt is three layers held together by thousands of stitches; you can't cut it, or you lose the whole point of using it. My hands hurt for two days after.

Drafting — a day, roughly. The cloth decided the shape. The quilt had a strong horizontal band through the middle, so the pattern got drafted around it. No size run, no grading — one body, one block, adjusted until the band landed where it needed to land.

You can't rush the part where the material tells you what it wants to be. That conversation takes as long as it takes.Studio note

Cutting and sewing — the bulk of it. Hand-finished armholes, bound edges, a lining pieced from the scraps so nothing from that quilt goes in the bin. Fittings in the middle, on a body, with pins.

Finishing — an afternoon. Number stamped. Moon phase recorded. Buttons from a jar of deadstock horn. Pressed, photographed, listed.

The number nobody likes

Weeks of labour, spread across a month of calendar, plus the looking. For one garment that can be sold exactly once.

A factory makes this arithmetic work by making two hundred of them — the setup cost collapses across the run. I have no run. Every single piece carries the full weight of its own setup, and there is no second unit to absorb it.

So the price will be what it is, and I've stopped framing it as a luxury. It's the cost of a thing being made once, by one person, from material that will never exist again. If that's not worth it to you, that's a real answer — and there are excellent people making beautiful things in batches at a fifth of the price. They're just not making this.

What I won't do is pretend the time isn't there. The honest number is weeks, not days. I'd rather tell you and let you decide than compress it into something that looks efficient and feels like nothing.

— BrendaLunary Studio

What "one of one" actually costs

Why a single garment can't be priced like a batch one — and what I'm choosing to be honest about instead.

I'm not going to post a fake ledger, because the first drop hasn't been priced yet and numbers I invent tonight would be a story, not a fact. What I can tell you is how the arithmetic of one-of-one works, so that when prices do appear you know exactly what you're looking at.

Materials

Not bought by the roll. Bought when found, in whatever quantity exists, at whatever the seller wants. There's no volume discount because there's no volume. The material cost of one piece is the full cost of the search that produced it, including the trips that produced nothing.

Labour

A batch maker spreads setup across two hundred units, so each one carries a sliver of the pattern, the samples, the mistakes. I spread it across one. Whatever the hours are, the whole thing lands on a single object — and that object can be sold exactly once.

A batch hides its costs inside the run. A one-of-one has nowhere to hide them, so it either charges honestly or it fails quietly.Studio note

What that means for the price

It will look high next to something made in a run, because it is carrying costs that a run gets to divide. That's not a markup on exclusivity. It's the absence of a second unit to absorb the setup.

Three things I'll commit to instead of a pricing table. Every listing will state the hours that went into the piece. Every listing will say what the material was and where it came from. And I won't run a fake "was" price to make the real one look like a deal.

What I'm not promising

That this is cheap, or that it's for everyone. There are excellent people making beautiful things in batches for a fifth of the money. They're just not making this — and if the honest number turns out to be more than the piece is worth to you, that's a real answer, not a failure of persuasion.

Prices arrive with the first drop, and I'd rather publish them late and true than early and invented.

— BrendaLunary Studio

The moon is the timestamp

Why every piece is stamped with a number and a phase, and what that mark is actually for.

Every piece that leaves here gets two marks: a sequential number, and the moon phase of the day it was finished. Both are stamped into the piece itself — inside a hem, under a cover board, on the back of a print — and both appear in the gallery.

People assume it's decoration. It's a record.

Why a number

The number is the piece's position in the whole body of work. Drop 003 is not "the third month" or "the third collection" — it's the third release, whatever it contained. One garment or six, it gets one number. That's the only way a sequence stays honest when the output is this irregular.

It also means the gallery can't be quietly edited later. If piece 17 exists, it exists. There's no version of the record where it didn't happen — which is why it starts empty rather than decorated.

Why the moon

Because a date is a bureaucratic fact and a phase is a felt one. I finish things at night, usually late, and the light outside the window is genuinely part of how I remember a piece. The full moon in June, the waning crescent in September — those are better handles for memory than 06.02.26.

There's also a practical reason: I don't work to a calendar. A phase is not a deadline. It tells you when something was done without implying when the next one is due.

What it does for you

When you own one of these, the mark is your proof of what it is — the specific object, the specific night. If you're here looking, the marks in the gallery are the only honest schedule this studio has. Read the gaps between them and you'll know exactly how erratic this is.

The gallery is not a catalogue. It's a log of nights when something got finished.Studio note

The next one gets stamped when it's done. That's as specific as I'm willing to be.

— BrendaLunary Studio

Deadstock isn't a marketing word

What it means when a roll of cloth is the last of its kind, and the constraint that comes with it.

Deadstock has become a label you can put on anything to make it sound responsible. In practice it means one specific thing: material that was manufactured, never used, and is no longer being made.

That's it. No virtue attached. Just a supply condition — and it makes everything downstream harder in ways worth explaining.

You can't reorder it

This is the whole constraint. When I find a roll of cloth, I buy all of it or I buy some of it, and whatever I don't buy disappears into someone else's project or a landfill. There is no restock. There is no "can you get more in a 34." There often isn't a fibre content label, and there's almost never a width I can plan around.

Which means the design process starts with an inventory problem, not an idea. I don't decide to make a coat and then find cloth. I find cloth and then work out what it's willing to become.

It's inconsistent, and that's structural

A mill end might be nine metres of perfect wool. The next one is two metres with a flaw down the middle. The two-metre piece becomes a bag, a collar, a set of pockets — or it doesn't become anything, and it goes back into the bin for another year.

This is why the gallery will look the way it does once it fills. Not a curated range, but a record of what the material allowed.

Everybody wants the story of the last roll of cloth. Nobody wants the two years of looking that made finding it possible.Studio note

Why I keep doing it

Because the alternative is ordering the same cloth from the same mill as everyone else, in whatever quantity I want, forever. That's a perfectly good way to run a clothing brand. It's just not a way to make something that couldn't have come from anyone else.

The constraint is the design. When the material runs out, the idea runs out with it, and something else begins.

— BrendaLunary Studio
